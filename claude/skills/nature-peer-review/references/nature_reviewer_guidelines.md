# Nature-Family Reviewer Guidelines (Synthesised)

This document distills publicly available reviewer guidance from Nature, Nature Medicine, Nature Communications, Nature Methods and Nature Biotechnology into a working reference for in-house use.

## What Nature-family editors expect from a reviewer

Per Nature's published guidance to reviewers ("How we look after the peer review process at Nature"), a useful reviewer report does five things:

1. **Verifies the central claim** — is the headline supported by the data shown?
2. **Identifies whether the work meets the journal's advance threshold** — significant cross-field interest vs solid specialist contribution
3. **Tests reproducibility and rigor** — could a reasonably equipped lab replicate this?
4. **Spots missing alternative explanations or controls** — what could account for the finding besides the proposed mechanism?
5. **Returns a clear, specific, actionable critique** — not a list of vague concerns

A common editor frustration is reviews that argue against publication without specifying what would change the reviewer's mind. Nature editors actively coach reviewers to write "minimum-experiment" comments: *the smallest additional experiment or analysis that would address the concern*.

## Calibrating the recommendation

Nature-family journals use four recommendations. Calibration differs from generic specialty journals.

| Recommendation | Generic-journal meaning | Nature-family meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Accept** | Paper is publication-ready | Paper is publication-ready AND clears advance threshold AND has no remaining major concerns. Rare on first review. |
| **Minor revision** | Small clarifications | Specific clarifications + 1–2 figure tweaks. No new experiments. Used when reviewer is confident the paper should be published if minor issues are fixed. |
| **Major revision** | Substantive changes needed | New analyses or experiments needed, OR substantial restructuring, OR a key claim needs to be walked back. Reviewer believes the paper *could* be acceptable after revision. |
| **Reject** | Paper is unsalvageable | EITHER (a) the work is technically flawed, OR (b) the advance does not meet the journal's threshold even if technically sound. The second case is the "reject for journal fit" — common at Nature, Nat Med, Nat Methods. |

### Specific calibration rules

- **"Reject" is appropriate** when the work is correct but better suited to a specialist journal. Editors expect reviewers to say so honestly.
- **"Major revision" requires that the reviewer believe the paper could clear the bar after revision.** If you would still recommend reject after the proposed revisions, recommend reject now.
- **"Minor revision" should not be used for new experiments.** If you want a new experiment, the correct recommendation is major revision.
- Do not soft-pedal a reject as a major revision. Editors find this unhelpful and authors find it cruel (they invest months to revise, then are rejected on second round).

## Common rejection reasons at Nature-family journals

Editors at Nature/Nat Med/Nat Comm cite the following as the dominant reasons for rejection at first review, in approximate frequency order:

1. **Advance is incremental.** Solid work, but does not extend the field meaningfully beyond a prior paper from the same group or a recent paper from another group.
2. **Claim is broader than data supports.** Mouse-only data with human-relevance claims; small-N pilot with clinical-translation claims.
3. **Alternative explanations not ruled out.** Particularly common in mechanism papers and in association studies.
4. **Reproducibility / methods clarity insufficient.** Inadequate methods detail; missing controls; missing CIs and effect sizes.
5. **Audience too narrow.** Excellent specialist contribution; wrong venue.
6. **Wrong format / framing.** Paper reads like a thesis chapter, not a Nature paper. Results sprawl across 8+ subsections.
7. **Inappropriate statistics.** Multiple testing not addressed; CIs absent; test assumptions not checked.

## Reviewer pitfalls Nature editors flag

Reviewers themselves have failure modes. Editors specifically warn against:

- **Reviewer #2 syndrome** — demanding experiments outside scope or relevance
- **Citation pushing** — recommending citation of the reviewer's own papers without strong justification
- **Methods nitpicking that does not affect the claim** — focusing on cosmetic methodological choices
- **Vague concerns** — "I am not convinced", "the analyses seem incomplete", with no specifics
- **Recycled language** — using boilerplate that does not engage with the manuscript
- **Conflicts of interest not disclosed** — competing work in pipeline; collaborator/family/competitor of authors
- **Polite-but-empty** — a review that praises the work and then recommends rejection with no actionable feedback

## What "high-quality" looks like in practice

A high-quality Nature-family review usually has:

- **2–4 major comments**, each tied to a specific claim and a specific fix
- **5–15 minor comments**, each location-specific
- **One paragraph of summary** at the top
- **One paragraph of advance assessment** that is honest about whether the work clears the bar
- **A reference-quality check** if anything looks thin
- **A clear, calibrated recommendation** that matches the body of the review

Word count is usually 800–2000 words. Reviews under 400 words look lazy; over 3000 words look unfocused.

## Two-stage review process at Nature-family journals

Many Nature-family journals operate a two-stage review:

1. **Round 1**: 3–4 reviewers comment. Editor synthesizes. If the paper is potentially salvageable, authors are invited to revise (the "transfer to revision" decision).
2. **Round 2**: Revised manuscript + author response → same reviewers re-read + comment on whether their concerns are addressed.

This means: **make round-1 comments explicit and falsifiable, because round-2 will explicitly ask "did the authors address concern X".** Vague concerns are unactionable for the authors and unsatisfying for the round-2 review.

## Confidentiality and ethics

- Reviews are confidential. Do not discuss the manuscript with anyone not directly involved in the review.
- Do not use information from the manuscript in your own work before publication.
- Disclose conflicts of interest immediately (collaborator within last 4 years, family relationship, direct competitor with similar paper in pipeline, financial interest).
- Do not delegate review to a colleague without informing the editor.
- Do not paste the manuscript into a general-purpose LLM. Nature-family journals' policy as of 2024–2025 explicitly prohibits this for confidentiality reasons. Reviewers may use journal-approved AI tools or run models locally.
